Comparison of Capital Stock Scaling

This dual visualisation explores the financial distribution of Brazilian companies by the role of their owners, using the national colors to segment the data into key quantiles (from the 25th to the 99th percentile). The Log Scale plot on the left provides a “balanced” view; by compressing the massive range of capital values, it reveals that nearly every owner role follows a similar internal hierarchy where the top 1% (q99) holds vastly more capital than the median (q50). This view allows us to learn about the roles with lower overall capital, such as Liquidators or Legal Representatives, which would otherwise be invisible.

Conversely, the Regular Scale plot on the right exposes the “absolute” economic reality of wealth concentration in Brazil. When viewed linearly, the logarithmic balancing effect vanishes, replaced by sharp spikes: most notably for Directors and President/Chairs. This comparison demonstrates that while the internal distribution of capital is skewed across all roles, the total volume of capital is dominated by a select few elite positions.

Wrangling and Prep Work

The raw CNPJ (Cadastro Nacional da Pessoa Jurídica) dataset contains highly detailed legal terminology that requires refinement before it can be visualised or understood. I performed several key transformations to prepare the data for the comparative analysis:

  • I removed the category Intervenor / Court-appointed Administrator to focus the analysis on standard business ownership and management roles rather than temporary legal interventions.
  • I renamed long, technical legal titles into concise equivalents to prevent the text from running off the screen and ensuring the polar plot remains readable.
  • Even after shortening, some labels remained long. I applied stringr::str_wrap with a width of 12 characters to ensure that the labels would sit neatly around the circular axis without overlapping or forcing the plot to shrink.
  • Because capital stock in Brazil is highly skewed, a simple mean would be misleading. Instead, I computed five specific percentiles: \(Q_{25}, Q_{50}, Q_{75}, Q_{95}, Q_{99}\).
  • Finally, I used pivot_longer to move the calculated quantiles into a long format so that we could stack the data in the plot.
Code 
quantile_data <- companies |> # removing intervenor
  filter(owner_qualification != "Intervenor / Court-appointed Administrator") |> 
  mutate(owner_qualification = case_match( # shortening names so that they are not cut in the plot
    owner_qualification,
    "Managing Partner / Partner-Administrator" ~ "Managing Partner",
    "Beneficial Owner (individual) resident or domiciled in Brazil" ~ "Resident Owner",
    "Administrator / Manager" ~ "Manager",
    "Entrepreneur / Business Owner" ~ "Entrepreneur",
    "President / Chair" ~ "President/Chair",
    "Director / Officer" ~ "Director",
    "Ostensible Partner (Managing partner in a silent partnership)" ~ "Silent Partner",
    "Sole Owner of an Individual Real Estate Company" ~ "Real Estate Owner",
    "Judicial Administrator (Court-appointed)" ~ "Court Admin",
    "Attorney-in-fact / Legal Representative (Power of Attorney)" ~ "Legal Rep",
    "Executor / Estate Administrator" ~ "Estate Admin",
    .default = owner_qualification # Keeps Liquidator as is
  )) |> 
  # wrap text - don't stretch the plot
  mutate(owner_qualification = stringr::str_wrap(owner_qualification, width = 12)) |> 
  group_by(owner_qualification) |> 
  summarise(q25 = quantile(capital_stock, p = 0.25), # computing percentiles for the plot
            q50 = quantile(capital_stock, p = 0.5),
            q75 = quantile(capital_stock, p = 0.75),
            q95 = quantile(capital_stock, p = 0.95),
            q99 = quantile(capital_stock, p = 0.99)) |> 
  pivot_longer(cols = q25:q99, names_to = "percentile", values_to = "quantile")

Brazilian Companies TidyTuesday Dataset

This dataset was obtained from the TidyTuesday GitHub Repository. This week we’re exploring Brazilian Companies, curated from Brazil’s open CNPJ (Cadastro Nacional da Pessoa Jurídica) records published by the Brazilian Ministry of Finance / Receita Federal on the national open-data portal (dados.gov.br).

The CNPJ open data is a large-scale public registry of Brazilian legal entities. For this dataset, the raw company records were cleaned and enriched with lookup tables (legal nature, owner qualification, and company size), then filtered to retain firms above a share-capital threshold so the analysis focuses on meaningful variation in capital stock.

Questions that the TidyTuesday community suggested we investiage is:

  • Which legal nature categories concentrate the highest total and average capital stock?
  • How does company size relate to capital stock (and how skewed is it)?
  • Do specific owner qualification groups dominate high-capital companies?
  • What patterns emerge when comparing the top capital-stock tail across categories (legal nature, size, qualification)?
