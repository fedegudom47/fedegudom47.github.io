What are hexbins?

Our choice of data visualisations are hexbins which are a way to represent dense 2d data sets by binning individual data points into hexagonal shapes with different hues. The colour of the hexagonal shapes denotes the number of points (in this case number of castles), fall within its area.

The benefits of hexbins include:

  • They prevent overplotting by summarising the density of an area
  • They reduce visual bias, they connect smoothly and therefore have less directional bias than other shapes (say if we plotted squares) since the distance to its neighbours is equal.
  • They work well with maps given that they can replace irregular geographic regions :D

Nothing too crazy here. We use geom_hex to plot the hexbins on a world map. We plot a world map below in order to help the viewer with their contextual awareness.

Looking Closer at Europe

Some light data-prep/wrangling

I’m lazy. Instead of manually separating the ISO codes present in this dataset, I used unique(world_castles$iso) to pull out all the names, and asked Gemini flash-lite to do the work for me. I then created a continent column using case_when().

    iso %in% europe_iso        ~ "Europe",

    iso %in% south_america_iso ~ "South America",
    iso %in% oceania_iso       ~ "Oceania",
    TRUE                       ~ "Other / Unknown"
  ))

What About Popularity?

So far every castle has counted for exactly one hexagon’s worth of colour, regardless of whether it’s the Great Wall of China or a deserted castle nobody has heard of. But the dataset gives us fame_rank (a blend of Wikipedia sitelinks and pageviews, where 1 is the most famous landmark on Earth).

Turning fame into weight

fame_rank is a rank, so a smaller number means more famous. To make popular castles pull harder on the map we flip it into a “fame weight”: the top-ranked castle gets the biggest weight, and the weight shrinks as we move down the ranking. Then we uncount() by that weight so a very famous castle contributes many points to the hexbin instead of just one.

Code 
n_castles <- max(world_castles$fame_rank, na.rm = TRUE)

world_castles_pop <- world_castles |>
  filter(!is.na(fame_rank)) |>
  mutate(fame_weight = round((n_castles - fame_rank + 1) / n_castles * 40) + 1) |>
  uncount(fame_weight)

Every castle still shows up at least once, but the famous ones now count for up to 40, so the hexbins lean towards where the well-known castles are rather the sheer count of castles.

About this Data

This data comes from Tidy Tuesday

This week we’re exploring the Castlemap dataset of 5,793 castles, fortresses, palaces and ruins in 138 countries. The data comes from Wikidata, and every landmark has verified coordinates, a Wikipedia article and a photo on Wikimedia Commons. Each also has a fame rank, based on how many languages have an article on it and how often that article is read. The Great Wall of China is first.

  • Which countries have the most castles?
  • Are palaces newer than fortresses?
  • Which landmarks have articles in many languages but few readers?

variable class description
qid character Wikidata item ID, e.g. Q1067425.
name character Landmark name, in English where available, e.g. Neuschwanstein Castle.
category character Type of landmark: castle, fortress, palace or ruin.
country character Country the landmark is in, e.g. France. The UK constituent countries are listed separately, e.g. Scotland.
iso character ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 country code, e.g. FR. The UK constituent countries use ISO 3166-2 subdivision codes, e.g. GB-SCT.

lon double Longitude in WGS84 decimal degrees, e.g. 2.12028.
year double Founding year, e.g. 1661. Negative for BC dates, e.g. -3000.
year_approx double Whether the founding year is an estimate, 1, or a documented date, 0.
century character Century the founding year falls in, e.g. 17th century or 30th century BC.
wikipedia character URL of the Wikipedia article, e.g. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Versailles.
image character URL of a Wikimedia Commons photo, e.g. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Alhambra%20detail.jpg?width=1280.
sitelinks double Number of Wikipedia language editions with an article on the landmark, e.g. 95.
pageviews double Wikipedia pageviews over the trailing 365 days, e.g. 756430.
fame_rank double Global fame rank, blending sitelinks and pageviews, where 1 is the most famous.
