When Officials Cooked the Books

In 2014, Flint, Michigan switched its water source to the Flint River to save money. Almost immediately, residents complained about discolored, foul-smelling water. By 2015, independent researchers and concerned residents began testing for lead contamination. Under the federal Lead and Copper Rule, if the 90th percentile of water samples exceeds 15 parts per billion (ppb), the water system must take corrective action. When initial tests showed Flint’s 90th percentile at a dangerous level well above this threshold, Michigan Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ) officials faced a crisis—not of public health, but of accountability. Their solution? Remove the highest-reading samples from the dataset on questionable technical grounds, including dismissing a sample of 104 ppb from activist Lee-Anne Walters’ home because she had requested a faucet filter weeks earlier. This wasn’t just poor judgment—it was deliberate data manipulation, a textbook case of cherry-picking results to reach a predetermined conclusion. By systematically excluding the worst contamination readings, officials manufactured a false sense of safety while residents, including thousands of children, continued drinking poisoned water. What follows is the visual story of how changing the data changed the outcome.
